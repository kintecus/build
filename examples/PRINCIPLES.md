# PM Principles

> Generated: 2026-02-17. A living document - update when your thinking evolves.

## How I think

Explore first, then structure. I don't reach for a framework immediately - I sit with the problem, poke at it, build intuition, then decide how much structure it actually needs. This means I sometimes move slower at the start, but I avoid applying the wrong framework to the wrong problem.

Best thinking happens solo with a doc - often starting as Obsidian notes or LLM conversations that get refined into something shareable. I need visuals to understand systems and relationships properly. Talking things through or whiteboarding comes second, as a validation step.

When stuck, external pressure unsticks me - a deadline, a commitment I've made, a push from a peer or manager. Internal motivation alone isn't always enough to break through, which means I need to engineer those external forcing functions deliberately.

## What I'm good at

**Hands-on breadth.** I operate across the stack in a way most PMs don't - SQL, Figma, terminal, data wrangling. This isn't dilettante dabbling; it's genuine technical interest that lets me go deep when the situation calls for it. Teams value this because I can actually read the code, question the architecture, and spec things in a way engineers respect.

**Responsibility and follow-through.** People trust me to own things. When teammates point to my contribution, it's usually reliability and breadth of knowledge - I cover a lot of ground and don't drop things.

**Finding the angle.** My best project outcomes happened when I cared enough (or was curious enough) to find a non-obvious point of view on a problem, develop a vision from it, and get buy-in from decision makers. Curiosity is the fuel - when I'm genuinely interested, the quality of my work goes up noticeably.

## How I like to work

**Minimum viable process:**

1. Single source of truth in one place (e.g., GitHub for both technical and project work)
2. Dailies - under 15 minutes, or async
3. A good kanban board

**Rituals I find useful:** Standups, planning (no further than 2 sprints ahead), weekly reports - preferably Amazon-style WBRs with data and narrative, not slide decks.

**Documentation:** I almost always write something down before acting, but I don't over-produce. Capture it, don't polish it. Match output weight to idea maturity.

**Communication:** Most comfortable with engineering, then leadership, then clients. I'm an introvert - large group presentations drain me. I prefer the Amazon "read the doc first, then discuss" format over live pitching to a room. I default to writing first (notes, async messages) and talking second.

**Visuals matter.** I reach for diagrams, system maps, and visual specs to understand and communicate. Short visual specs over long prose.

## What goes wrong

**Over-scoping when proving value.** Kicks in during the first 90 days on a new team, or any situation where I feel I need to justify my presence. I take on too much to demonstrate breadth instead of focusing on the highest-leverage thing.

**Over-engineering process when role is unclear.** When I'm unsure what I'm supposed to be doing, I default to building process infrastructure. Sometimes that's useful. Often it's avoidance.

**Freeze and overthink.** My worst instinct under stress. Instead of talking to people or asking for help, I retreat into my head, loop on the problem, and get stuck. The strength (deep thinking) becomes the trap.

**High-stakes presentation anxiety.** I want my ideas approved, but the process of presenting to a large group costs me disproportionate energy. This can lead to over-preparation or avoidance.

**Grudge risk.** When I disagree with a decision and lose, I've historically held onto it longer than useful. I've gotten better at recognizing this, but it's still a pattern to watch.

**Burnout signals (early):** Stress without proportional cause. Increased procrastination. Reaching for coping mechanisms after a period of not needing them. By the time I notice, I'm usually already in it - external observation helps.

## Influences

**Build (Tony Fadell):** Great things are built by exceptional teams with great leadership. I take the team-building and craft principles seriously. I don't care about his scale of success - the principles apply at any size.

**Shape Up (Basecamp):** The whole system resonates - appetite over estimates, no long-term roadmaps, pitching then cutting 6-week cycles, small autonomous groups. This is closest to how I naturally want to work.

**Design of Everyday Things (Don Norman):** Design is how it works, not how it looks. User-centered thinking as a default lens.

**Rework (37 Signals):** Do less, but do it well. Question whether you need the thing before building it.

**Steve Jobs / Apple HIG:** Taste matters. Sweat the details. Human interface design as a discipline, not decoration.

**Amazon operating principles:** Working backwards, writing excellence, WBRs, team charters. The writing culture especially - forcing clarity of thought through narrative.

## In one sentence

The job is navigating the tension between delighting your customer, achieving company targets, and doing it within the budget - and being honest about the trade-offs when you can't do all three.
